On self-learning systems

I spent a summer trying to pin down what people actually mean by a system that "learns on its own" — and came out the other side with a much narrower definition and one idea I've been building on ever since.

the itch

"Self-learning" gets used for almost anything. A model fitting parameters on new data gets called self-learning. So does a system that rewrites how it learns. Those are very different things and lumping them together makes it impossible to talk about either properly, so I wanted to separate them.

The distinction I settled on: most systems adjust parameters inside a learning process someone else fixed. The genuinely interesting ones adjust the process. Almost everything marketed as the second is the first.

the maths I had to go and learn

I was writing this alongside Imperial's AIAI1 course on algorithmic information theory, and that gave me the vocabulary I was missing. Once you start thinking about learning as compression — a good model is a short description of the data — a lot of vague questions get sharper. "Has it learned something?" becomes "has the description got shorter?", and that's a question with an answer.

I'll be honest that this was the hardest part. I came at it from a software engineering degree, not a maths one, and a fair bit of that summer was spent rereading the same pages until they clicked. Working through Bishop properly afterwards was a direct consequence.

the idea that survived

Most of what I wrote was throat-clearing. One idea came out intact: if learning is compression, then allocate computation by approximating minimum description length — spend compute where it buys you the most description.

That sentence turned into BidNet, where experts bid for their own compute through a differentiable auction and the network only runs the specialists worth running. Roughly 3× less compute for comparable accuracy, and around 60% activation sparsity with specialisation nobody designed in. I like that the theory produced an architecture rather than the other way round — that's the order I want to keep working in.

what I'd do differently

It's an investigative piece, not an experimental one, and I'd write it with more empirical backbone now — fewer pages establishing definitions, more runs testing them. But it did the job I needed it to do, which was to force me to actually understand the thing rather than gesture at it.
